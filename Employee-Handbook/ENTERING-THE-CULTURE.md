# Entering the Culture — An Employee Journey at Basic City

**Working Draft 0.1 — November 6, 2026**  
**Status:** Exploratory handbook architecture, **not** an adopted training plan or personnel policy  
**Governing reference:** [Cultural Constitution — Draft 0.1](CULTURAL-CONSTITUTION.md) (**protected; no edits without explicit approval**)  
**Interpretive reference:** [Constitution Companion](CONSTITUTION-COMPANION.md)

## Why this document exists

Craft Sublime is a philosophy larger than Basic City. Basic City is one place where it takes form in beer, food, music, hospitality, technical work and human encounters.

The constitution describes **what we cultivate**. This document explores **how someone experiences and participates in that cultivation** from their first contact with the organization onward.

This is not a new ladder of employee ranks. Nor is it an attempt to produce a prescribed emotional or spiritual outcome. A person may learn the work, disagree with an interpretation, contribute fully and leave without accepting the philosophy as a personal worldview.

The working question is:

> What must the place, the people and the work make available so someone can enter, learn, contribute, become capable, help others and leave something of value behind?

## The journey is relational, not a score

The Foundation's formation arc—**Visitor → Initiate → Regular → Insider → Steward**—describes possible relationships to a practice. Its companion, *The Becoming*, emphasizes what happens **between** these stations: participation, fluency, trust, relinquishment and renewal. In this project we use the arc as a **design lens**, not an HR classification, certification pathway, pay scale or personality judgment.

An employee may move between modes depending on the situation: a long-time brewer can be a visitor to the Foundry's production systems; a new hire can practice stewardship by helping another person. No one is “more valuable” because of a station label.

## The proposed experience

### 1. Arrival — Being received

**Employee's experience:** “I can enter this place, find my footing, know who is here to help me, and understand what I am joining without being expected to already belong.”

**What Basic City must make available (proposals to verify):**
- A human welcome and clear point of contact
- Accurate, usable information about role, schedule, pay, safety, workplace expectations and who answers questions
- A physical orientation to the campus, relevant workspace and people
- Permission to ask questions and describe what seems unclear or unsafe
- The possibility of encountering the place's story through something tangible, not merely a recital of values

**Possible cultural expression:** An experienced coworker shows one real thing they have learned to tend—a tap system, recipe, stage transition, shared space or guest ritual—and why it matters.

**Source connection:** Constitution I, II and V; Maker–Made Thing–Receiver; culture as tending.

**Not a claim of existing policy:** The exact orientation schedule and assigned ownership are to be checked with managers.

### 2. Apprenticeship — Learning to make and receive

**Employee's experience:** “I am learning the craft itself, and also how to notice what the work is asking of me.”

**What the organization and coworkers practice:**
- Demonstrate the real work before teaching its mythology
- Explain what good execution looks like, and why
- Allow supported repetition, feedback and correction
- Show how to seek help rather than improvise unsafe work
- Make learning bidirectional: a new employee can observe what long-time staff overlook

**Proposed practical prompt:** “What am I making? What am I receiving? What do I need to learn to notice?”

**Source connection:** Constitution III and V; Two Hands and Transparent Relay; Plain Glossary.

**Guardrail:** Work standards and safety competency must be objective and role-specific. “Tuning” is a metaphor for attentive practice, not an alternative to training or safe procedure.

### 3. Participation — Becoming part of a relay

**Employee's experience:** “My work matters because someone else can receive it and carry it forward.”

**What this may look like:**
- Clear handoffs between people, shifts and departments
- Recognizing what another team needs before a shift or event
- Maintaining shared tools, spaces and records
- Giving accurate information to the next worker
- Recognizing the unseen work upstream of a guest's experience

**Possible campus example to verify:** A successful show-night exchange between Foundry production, bar staff, kitchen and front-of-house staff. The form is not one department servicing another; it is a coordinated encounter in which each craft contributes.

**Source connection:** Constitution IV; Grammar of the Relay; Law of Coherence.

**Guardrail:** Cooperation does not cancel defined authority or professional accountability.

### 4. Fluency — Developing judgment

**Employee's experience:** “I can do the work with skill and begin to tell when the usual approach is not enough.”

This is more than repetition. The work acquires discernment: reading the situation, identifying a change, adapting without violating standards, and recognizing when a familiar practice has stopped serving its purpose.

**Possible practices (to validate):**
- Pre-shift read of the specific day, team and guest situation
- Prompt identification of an emerging issue
- A concise post-shift observation and handoff
- Coaching by explanation rather than status

**Questions worth asking:** “What is actually happening? What needs attention? What should we build? What should we clear?”

**Source connection:** Constitution III, VII; the Field Kit's emphasis on giving ideas tangible form; distinction between routine and attentive practice.

**Guardrail:** Reading the room is not permission to assume another person's feelings or to override established requirements.

### 5. Stewardship — Making capacity available

**Employee's experience:** “What I know does not have to end with me.”

The source arc locates an important turn here: real capability matures when someone can share it without demanding ownership of the result.

**Possible expressions:**
- Teaching a coworker enough to act independently
- Improving an instruction so the next person does not have to reconstruct it
- Recognizing and crediting others' contributions
- Raising a difficult truth before it becomes a larger problem
- Leaving a shift, task or workspace in a condition that helps the next participant

**Source connection:** Constitution VI, VII and VIII; Identity Progression, The Becoming, Valere, Regeneration.

**Guardrail:** Stewardship is a practice, **not** a title employees must earn or a surrogate for promotion, compensation or personal worth. Legitimate management authority remains explicit and separate.

### 6. Departure and return — What remains

An employee's participation may end. The culture is tested not only by what happens while people work here but also by how transitions are handled and what knowledge, relationships and capacities remain.

**Working questions:**
- Can essential knowledge be handed over without blame or secrecy?
- Can people leave with dignity?
- What did this person make possible for others?
- What did the organization learn from their experience?

**Source connection:** Constitution VIII and IX; Valere and Regeneration.

**Guardrail:** Actual separation and final-pay practices belong in the reviewed employment framework, not in a philosophical metaphor.

## A rhythm rather than a ceremony

The philosophy becomes credible through repeatable action, not compulsory ritual. A candidate rhythm to test with staff:

| Moment | Purpose | Plain-language question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Before work | Prepare and orient | What is happening today, and what matters most? |
| During work | Attend, make, receive | What is needed now? |
| Handoff | Carry work forward | What must the next person know? |
| After work | Observe the trace | What worked, what did not, and what should change? |

**This rhythm is a design proposal, not a verified current Basic City practice.** It must be checked against existing department routines and workload.

## What employees should not be asked to do

An employee should not have to:
- Accept metaphysical propositions as personal beliefs
- Recite theory as a substitute for executing the job
- Perform enthusiasm or claim a transformative experience
- Treat a developmental metaphor as evidence of their human worth
- Stay silent about unsafe conditions, mistakes or disagreement to protect the story of the culture
- Take personal responsibility for organizational failures outside their control

These are proposed boundaries for handbook development, consistent with the constitution's invitation, reciprocity and integrity commitments. They require implementation and policy review before representing them as adopted company protections.

## What we need to learn from Basic City

Before drafting an actual orientation and training chapter, confirm:
1. What a new employee currently receives and experiences before and on their first shift
2. How training differs in Brewery, Taproom, Foundry, Fitchen, Showroom and Sip City
3. Which handoffs are critical, and where they regularly break
4. Which historical practices from previous handbooks still exist, which have changed and which were only aspirations
5. Who can provide or verify each operational fact
6. Which employee feedback methods protect candor without creating additional surveillance or subjective ranking

The older handbooks are **source material for examples and operational substance only**; they do not determine the design of this journey.

## Decisions for review

- Is **“Entering the Culture”** the right employee-facing title, or does it sound more like an internal design document?
- Should the journey be presented through a **single first-day narrative** or through several examples across departments?
- What is the clearest way to show the developmental arc without making it an explicit stage system for employees?
- What is the minimum credible set of practices Basic City can actually support?
